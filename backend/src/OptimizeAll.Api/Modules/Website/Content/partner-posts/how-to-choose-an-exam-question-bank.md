For most professional exams, practice questions are where preparation is won or lost. Reading builds understanding, but only answering questions builds the skill the exam actually tests. So the choice of an exam question bank matters more than many candidates realise, and yet it is often made on impulse: a recommendation in a forum, an attractive landing page, a free trial that expires.

This guide gives you six criteria, a scoring table you can reuse for any credential, a list of red flags and a short method for running a fair trial. It does not rank products. The aim is to help you decide with evidence.

> Exam content, formats and rules are set by the official body for each credential and change over time. Check the official body's current requirements and published syllabus, and use them as the yardstick against which any question bank is judged.

## What a question bank is for

A good bank does four jobs:

1. **Reveals gaps** by showing which topics you cannot yet apply.
2. **Builds fluency** with the exam's style of question.
3. **Trains timing and stamina** through timed sets and mock exams.
4. **Provides feedback** in the explanations, which are where most learning happens.

If a bank does the first three but not the fourth, you will know that you are wrong without learning why. So explanation quality sits near the top of any scoring table.

## Six criteria that matter

### 1. Blueprint mapping

Does the bank follow the official exam outline? Questions should be tagged to the topics in the body's current syllabus, so you can see your coverage and weak areas by blueprint area. Ask how and when the mapping is updated, and whether the topic labels match the official ones. A bank that is not anchored to the syllabus may over-practise easy topics and miss the hard ones.

### 2. Explanation quality

A good explanation says why the right answer is right and why each wrong option fails. It cites the standard or principle, shows working for calculations and teaches the method rather than just stating the result. Sample a handful of explanations on questions you got wrong and ask: would I have learnt something if I had not known the answer?

### 3. Verification and authorship

Who writes the questions, and how are they checked? Questions written and reviewed by qualified professionals are more likely to be accurate and realistic than those assembled without expertise. Look for a clear description of the review process. Errors in a question bank are costly because you may memorise a wrong rule.

### 4. Personalisation and adaptive practice

Can the bank direct you toward your weak areas, rather than offering the same pool in a fixed order? Personalised practice saves time, which matters most for working candidates. Check that you can still choose your own topics and timed sets, since you should not hand all control to an algorithm.

### 5. Coaching and support

Does it help you think when you are stuck? Static explanations are useful but cannot answer your follow-up question. A good coaching feature asks questions, helps you find the error in your reasoning and does not simply hand over the answer. See [how to use an AI study coach without outsourcing thinking](/blog/how-to-use-an-ai-study-coach) for how to judge this.

### 6. Mock exam realism

Are the mock exams close to the real exam in length, timing, interface and question style? Does the bank turn off help during mocks, so the result is a fair measure? A mock that you can complete with hints is not a mock. Our guide to [reviewing a mock exam](/blog/how-to-review-a-mock-exam) explains what to do with the result.

## A scoring table

Score each bank from 0 to 5 on each criterion, multiply by the weight that reflects your situation and add up. The weights below are **illustrative**; change them to suit you.

| Criterion | Weight | Bank A score (0-5) | Bank B score (0-5) | Notes to record |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Blueprint mapping | 3 | | | Are topics tagged to the official outline? |
| Explanation quality | 3 | | | Do explanations teach the method? |
| Verification and authorship | 3 | | | Who writes and reviews? |
| Personalised practice | 2 | | | Can it target weak topics? |
| Coaching and support | 2 | | | Does it prompt thinking? |
| Mock exam realism | 3 | | | Timed, realistic, no help? |

The weighted total is a conversation starter, not a verdict. If two banks are close, let the trial results break the tie. If you are a working candidate with little time, raise the weight on personalisation. If you tend to rely on hints, raise the weight on mock realism.

### A filled example

An **illustrative** scoring for a candidate, with made-up scores:

- Blueprint mapping 4 times weight 3 equals 12
- Explanation quality 5 times 3 equals 15
- Verification 4 times 3 equals 12
- Personalised practice 3 times 2 equals 6
- Coaching 4 times 2 equals 8
- Mock realism 4 times 3 equals 12

The total is 65 out of a possible 80. The number matters less than what it exposes: here, personalisation is the weakest area, and the candidate might compensate by choosing topic sets carefully.

## Red flags

- **No link to the official syllabus,** or topic names that do not match it.
- **Explanations that only state the answer.**
- **No information about who writes or checks questions.**
- **Claims that cannot be verified,** such as vague promises about results. Be sceptical of any resource that promises outcomes, because no resource can control your effort or the exam.
- **Questions that are far easier or odder than the real exam style,** which gives false comfort.
- **Mocks with hints on,** or no timing.
- **Out-of-date content,** especially where standards or exam formats have changed.
- **Memorisable question pools** with no variety, which let you pass practice by recognising items.

## Run a fair trial

Before committing, spend a week on a trial and test it deliberately.

1. **Take a diagnostic set** across the blueprint and see whether the topic labels make sense.
2. **Choose five questions you know you got wrong elsewhere** and read the explanations.
3. **Try the support tools,** asking a follow-up question and noticing whether the answer guides or gives away.
4. **Take a timed set** and check the interface, timer and review screens.
5. **Look for errors.** If you spot an error, see how the provider handles feedback.
6. **Check cost against your plan** in your own currency and timeline, using the provider's current published terms, since offers change.
7. **Use it on the device you will actually study with.** If most of your sessions happen on a phone during a commute, test the mobile experience, not just the desktop one, and check that your progress and error data follow you across devices.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many question banks do I need?

Usually one main bank is enough, and it is better to work through it thoroughly than to spread effort across many. Add the official body's sample questions to check that your main bank matches the real style.

### Are free questions good enough?

Some free material is useful for sampling, but check it against the same criteria: syllabus mapping, explanation quality and who checks the questions.

### Should I choose the bank with the most questions?

Not by size alone. Quality, mapping and explanation matter more than volume, though you do need enough questions to practise each topic without repeating items.

### How do I know a bank is accurate?

You cannot be sure, but you can look for a described review process, test explanations against the official syllabus and standards, and see how the provider responds if you report an error.
