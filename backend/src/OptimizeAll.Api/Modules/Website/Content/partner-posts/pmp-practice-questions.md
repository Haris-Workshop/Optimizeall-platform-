Ask people who passed the PMP what made the difference and many will say the same thing: practice questions. But ask people who struggled and you will often hear that they did plenty of questions too. The difference is rarely the number of questions answered. It is **how** they were used.

This guide explains how to use **PMP practice questions** to build the judgement the exam rewards: when to start, how many to do, how to review, how to track weaknesses and how to avoid the trap of memorising a question bank. It applies to any good question bank.

Before choosing questions, check that they match the official requirements of the current PMP exam content outline published by PMI.

## Why practice questions matter so much for the PMP

The PMP is mostly situational. Rather than asking you to define a term, a question typically describes a project situation and asks what the project manager should do, often what they should do **first**. Several answers may be reasonable; one is best given the context and PMI's view of good practice.

That kind of judgement cannot be learned by reading alone. It is built by repeatedly making decisions, getting feedback and understanding why the best answer is best. Practice questions are the feedback loop.

## When to start

Start earlier than feels comfortable. A common mistake is to finish all the reading before touching questions. Instead:

- **Week 1:** a short diagnostic set to see where you stand.
- **From week 2:** topic-based questions immediately after studying each topic.
- **From the midpoint:** mixed sets that combine topics, as the exam does.
- **Final third:** full-length timed mock exams.

Our [8-week PMP study plan](/blog/pmp-study-plan) shows how this fits into a full schedule.

## How many questions?

There is no official number. Many successful candidates answer somewhere between several hundred and well over a thousand questions. More important than the total:

- **Review every question**, including the ones you got right.
- **Cover every domain and approach** — predictive, agile and hybrid — in proportion to the exam content outline.
- **Do at least two or three full-length timed mocks** before the exam.

If you have done 1,500 questions but reviewed them quickly, you have probably learned less than someone who did 600 and reviewed each one carefully.

## The review method that makes the difference

For every question, spend at least as long reviewing as answering. Use four questions:

1. **What was really being asked?** Underline the key words: *first*, *next*, *best*, *most likely*, *should*. A question asking for the first action has a different answer from one asking for the best long-term solution.
2. **What is the situation?** Identify the life cycle (predictive, agile, hybrid), the project phase and who is involved.
3. **Why is the correct answer best?** Link it to a principle — for example, "address conflict directly and collaboratively before escalating" or "assess the impact before taking action on a change request".
4. **Why are the other options worse?** This is where most learning happens. Often a wrong option is a good action at the wrong time, or something the project manager should not do alone.

### Example

*A team member tells the project manager that a key stakeholder has been bypassing the agreed change process by asking developers directly for new features. What should the project manager do first?*

- A. Escalate the issue to the sponsor.
- B. Meet the stakeholder to understand their needs and explain the change process.
- C. Instruct developers to ignore requests outside the process.
- D. Update the stakeholder register.

**Best answer: B.** The stakeholder is acting outside the process, probably because their needs are not being met. Engaging them directly and collaboratively addresses the root cause. Escalation (A) is premature. Instructing developers (C) may be needed later but does not solve the stakeholder issue. Updating the register (D) is useful but does not address the behaviour.

The principle for the error log: *engage directly and collaboratively first; escalate only if direct action fails.*

## Keep an error log

An error log turns mistakes into learning. A simple table works:

| Date | Topic | Question summary | Why I got it wrong | Principle to apply |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 12 May | Stakeholders | Stakeholder bypassing change process | Chose escalation | Engage directly first |
| 13 May | Agile | Team missing sprint goal | Chose to add resources | Let the team self-organise; inspect and adapt at retrospective |

Review the log weekly. Patterns appear quickly: you may find that you consistently escalate too early, or that you favour predictive answers in agile scenarios. Those patterns are worth more than any single topic review.

## Avoid memorising the bank

If you see the same questions repeatedly, you will start recognising answers rather than reasoning. Signs this is happening: scores rising quickly on repeated sets but not on new ones. To avoid it:

- use a bank large enough that repetition is limited;
- reset and re-attempt questions only after a gap of several weeks;
- prioritise new questions in your weak areas;
- take mocks from questions you have not seen before.

## Use mocks to practise timing

Full-length mocks train stamina and pacing, not just knowledge. Plan your time per section, practise flagging and moving on, and use any breaks the exam allows. ## Using hints and coaching without becoming dependent

Many modern question banks offer hints, explanations on demand or an AI tutor. They are useful, but they can also become a crutch. A few habits keep you learning:

- **Commit to an answer first.** Choose your answer before asking for help. The act of committing makes the feedback more memorable.
- **Ask for reasoning, not the answer.** A good tutor asks you questions that lead you to the principle. If you just ask "which is right?", you skip the learning.
- **Explain it back.** After a hint, explain in one sentence why the best answer is best. If you cannot, you have not learned it yet.
- **Wean off gradually.** Use help freely in topic practice, sparingly in mixed sets and not at all in timed mocks.

The aim is to rehearse, in practice, the reasoning you will need to do alone in the exam.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are free PMP practice questions good enough?

Some are, but quality varies widely. Look for questions aligned to the current exam content outline, with clear explanations of why each option is right or wrong. Outdated or poorly explained questions can teach the wrong habits.

### Should I time myself on every practice set?

Not at first. Untimed practice is better while learning a topic. Introduce timing in mixed sets and always in full mocks.

### What score should I aim for in practice?

Different banks are calibrated differently, so there is no universal target. Look for consistent results on unseen questions across several mocks, and compare with any guidance your question bank provides.

### Is it bad to get many questions wrong at the start?

No. Early mistakes are expected and useful. What matters is that you understand each one and stop repeating the same type of error.

### Should I use more than one PMP question bank?

One high-quality bank aligned to the current outline is usually enough, supplemented by PMI's official sample materials. A second bank can help near the end if you have exhausted unseen questions in the first.
