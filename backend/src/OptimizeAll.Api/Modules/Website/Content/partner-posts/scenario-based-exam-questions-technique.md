Professional exams have moved steadily from asking what a rule says to asking what you would do. Scenario-based exam questions describe a situation with a cast of characters, constraints and complications, then ask you to decide. They feel harder than recall questions because the answer is not in the question; you have to build it. They are also more learnable than they look, because the same four moves work across accounting, audit, project management and beyond.

This article sets out that method, parse, decide, eliminate and justify, and applies it to three short worked examples. It also explains why the same skill matters for scenario-based professional certifications such as those issued by PCI AI.

> Formats, scoring and rules differ by exam and are set by each official body. Check the official body's current requirements: for example, AICPA and NASBA for the CPA, the IIA for the CIA, PMI for the PMP and pciai.org for PCI AI certifications.

## Why scenarios are different

A recall question rewards memory. A scenario question rewards judgement under ambiguity. Three features define the challenge.

- **Noise.** Scenarios include details that do not matter, and a few that matter a great deal.
- **Plausible options.** The wrong answers are usually things that someone could reasonably do, but not first, not here or not on their own.
- **Implicit criteria.** The best answer is judged against professional standards, principles or good practice, rather than your employer's habits.

## The four-move method

### Move 1: Parse

Read the last sentence of the question first, so you know what you are being asked to produce. Then read the scenario and mark three things: the role you are in, the facts that constrain the decision and the qualifier in the question, such as first, best, most likely or not.

### Move 2: Decide the decision

Before looking at options, state in one sentence what decision is being tested. For example: whether revenue is recognised now or later; whether the auditor can rely on a control; whether a change request should be approved. This is the most important move because it stops the options from steering you.

### Move 3: Eliminate

Now read the options and remove those that fail a test. Useful tests include: does it answer the question asked, does it respect the role and its authority, does it ignore a stated fact, is it premature or too extreme, and does it contradict the principle in play. Aim to eliminate two quickly, then compare the remaining pair.

### Move 4: Justify

Choose the best remaining option and write one phrase of justification, in your head or on scrap paper: because X is the first step or because the risk is Y. If you cannot justify it in a phrase, go back to move two.

## Worked example 1: accounting

The scenario is **fictional and illustrative**.

*Orchard Ltd sells equipment and a two-year service plan together for one price. The service plan could be bought separately from other providers, and the customer can use the equipment without it. How should the entity account for the sale?*

- A. Recognise all revenue on delivery of the equipment.
- B. Treat the equipment and service plan as separate promises, allocate the price based on standalone selling prices and recognise each as it is satisfied.
- C. Defer all revenue until the service plan ends.
- D. Recognise the equipment at its list price and ignore the plan.

**Parse:** the role is the accountant, and the key facts are that the service is sold separately by others and the equipment works without it. **Decision:** is this one performance obligation or two? **Eliminate:** A and C treat the sale as a single package, which the facts contradict. D ignores the allocation. **Justify:** B, because the facts show the promises are distinct. ## Worked example 2: audit

*An internal auditor, reviewing the purchasing function, finds that the same employee raises purchase orders and approves supplier payments. Management says it is a small team and the employee is trusted. What should the auditor do?*

- A. Accept management's explanation because the employee is trusted.
- B. Report the lack of segregation of duties as a control weakness and discuss compensating controls with management.
- C. Ask the employee to resign.
- D. Ignore the finding since no loss has occurred.

**Parse:** role is auditor; fact is one person controls two incompatible steps. **Decision:** how should a control weakness be handled? **Eliminate:** A relies on trust, which is not a control. C is outside the auditor's authority. D ignores risk, which exists even without a loss. **Justify:** B, because the auditor reports the weakness and explores compensating controls. ## Worked example 3: project management

*A project manager on a predictive project learns that a supplier will deliver a key component two weeks late. The delay would affect the critical path. What should the project manager do first?*

- A. Inform the sponsor that the project will be late.
- B. Assess the impact on the schedule and explore options such as re-sequencing or crashing, then update the plan and communicate.
- C. Ask the team to work overtime on all tasks.
- D. Wait to see if the supplier recovers.

**Parse:** the role is project manager; the fact is that a critical-path item is delayed. **Decision:** what is the first response to a threat to the schedule? **Eliminate:** A reports before analysing. C applies a blunt remedy to non-critical work. D is passive. **Justify:** B, because analysis precedes escalation and the response focuses on the critical path. ## Common errors in scenario questions

- **Answering before deciding.** You read the options and gravitate to the first that sounds good.
- **Using your workplace instead of the standard.** The exam describes good practice, not local habit.
- **Choosing the most dramatic action.** The best answer is usually proportionate.
- **Missing the qualifier.** First is not the same as best.
- **Overreading.** Do not invent facts that are not in the scenario.

## How to train the method

1. **Do small daily sets** of five to ten scenarios.
2. **Write the decision sentence** for each before looking at options.
3. **Name why each wrong option fails,** using a short tag such as premature, wrong role, ignores fact or too extreme.
4. **Keep an error log** and look for tags that recur.
5. **Rewrite the scenario with one fact changed** and see whether the answer changes. This builds flexible understanding.
6. **Practise written scenarios too.** Where the exam asks for an essay, the same parse and decide moves apply, as in [CMA essay questions](/blog/cma-essay-questions-technique).
7. **Choose practice material that explains its reasoning,** as covered in [how to choose an exam question bank](/blog/how-to-choose-an-exam-question-bank).

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should I spend on a scenario question?

Check your exam's timing, then set a per-question allowance and a flagging rule. The method itself should take well under a minute for most items once practised.

### Should I read the options before the scenario?

Read the final question first, then the scenario, then decide the decision in your own words before reading the options. Reading options first lets them frame your thinking.

### What if two options both look right?

Return to the qualifier and the role. One option is usually premature, incomplete or outside authority. If you still cannot decide, choose the one that addresses the stated problem most directly.

### Do these methods apply to every professional exam?

The four moves are general, but the criteria for the best answer differ by profession. Study each exam's official standards and sample questions.
