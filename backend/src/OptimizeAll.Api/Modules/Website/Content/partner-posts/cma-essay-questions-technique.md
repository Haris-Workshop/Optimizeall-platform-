Many CMA candidates spend months mastering calculations and then meet the written part of the exam with no method at all. CMA essay questions reward a different skill: communicating accounting judgement clearly, under time pressure, to someone marking against a list of points. The good news is that written answers are one of the most trainable parts of the exam, because the structure is repeatable.

This guide sets out a structure that earns partial credit, shows a worked illustrative answer, explains how to think like the marker, and offers a timing plan.

> The exam format, the number and weighting of essay questions, scoring and any rules on answers are set by the Institute of Management Accountants (IMA) and can change. Check the official body's current requirements and sample questions before relying on any description here.

## How essays are likely to be marked

You will not see the marking scheme, but you can reason about its shape. Essay answers are generally assessed against a set of expected points: correct identification of the issue, appropriate use of concepts or calculations, a justified conclusion and clear communication. That has two practical consequences.

- **Marks accumulate point by point.** Each valid, relevant point can earn credit, so a long paragraph that makes one point earns less than four short sentences that make four.
- **Relevance beats volume.** Marks are not awarded for padding, and unsupported opinions earn little.

This is why a structure matters. It helps you place each point where the marker expects to find it.

## A five-part structure: I-C-A-J-R

Use a repeatable skeleton and fill it in quickly.

1. **Issue.** State in one sentence what the question is really asking.
2. **Concept.** Name the relevant technique, principle or standard.
3. **Application.** Apply it to the facts given, using figures and details from the scenario.
4. **Judgement.** Weigh the evidence, including a limitation or alternative where it is relevant.
5. **Recommendation.** Finish with a clear answer or action.

Each part is short. The point of the skeleton is to ensure that no marking category is left empty.

## Worked example with partial credit

The question below is **fictional and illustrative**.

*Harbour Components is considering whether to accept a one-off order at a price below its normal selling price. Spare capacity exists. The finance director asks you to explain whether the order should be accepted and what other factors should be considered. Variable cost per unit is 40, normal selling price is 70 and the special order price is 52.*

### A strong answer

- **Issue:** The decision is whether the special order improves profit and what non-financial factors matter.
- **Concept:** This is a relevant-cost, contribution-based decision. With spare capacity, fixed costs do not change, so they are not relevant.
- **Application:** Contribution per unit on the special order is 52 minus 40, which is 12, so the order adds contribution of 12 per unit and increases profit if capacity is genuinely spare and fixed costs are unaffected.
- **Judgement:** The order is attractive financially, but risks include existing customers learning of the lower price, a precedent for further price cuts, and whether capacity might be needed for full-price orders later.
- **Recommendation:** Accept, provided the order does not displace full-price sales and the price cut is contained, for example by a one-off, non-repeating agreement.

### How partial credit might arise

Imagine a marker with these scoring points: identifies relevant costs, calculates contribution, notes spare capacity assumption, discusses pricing risk, gives a recommendation. A candidate who only calculated 12 per unit would earn the calculation point and perhaps the relevant-cost point. A candidate who calculated nothing but wrote a sound discussion of risks and a recommendation would earn those points. The structure helps you collect all five rather than one or two. ## Thinking like the marker

Before you write, ask three questions.

- **What does the verb demand?** Calculate, explain, evaluate and recommend are different tasks. Evaluate means weigh both sides. Recommend means commit to a decision.
- **What facts did the examiner put in the scenario?** Details are rarely random. A mention of spare capacity, a long-term customer or a regulation is usually a prompt for a point.
- **What would a sceptical colleague challenge?** The limitation or assumption you note may be exactly the point the marker has listed.

### Common ways to lose marks

- Describing the theory without applying it to the facts.
- Making a calculation without stating what it means.
- Listing points with no explanation.
- Ignoring the final instruction, such as a recommendation.
- Running out of time and leaving the last part blank.

## A timing plan

The right allowance depends on the current exam format, so check it with the IMA. Once you know the time available per essay, split it. As an illustration only, a short essay might be allocated this way:

| Stage | Share of time | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| Read and plan | About 15 percent | Underline the verb and facts; jot headings from I-C-A-J-R |
| Write | About 70 percent | Fill each heading in short, specific sentences |
| Check | About 15 percent | Confirm the recommendation exists and figures agree |

Practise to a timer from the start. Candidates who write untimed essays during the learning phase are often surprised by how much less they can write in the real allowance.

## Practising essay answers

1. **Collect past-style questions** from your question bank or study materials.
2. **Plan before writing.** Spend two minutes drafting headings from the skeleton.
3. **Write to the clock.**
4. **Self-mark with a rubric.** List the points a marker might expect, then tick those you made. Be strict.
5. **Rewrite weak answers a few days later** without looking at the model.
6. **Keep a points bank.** For each topic, note typical issues and typical limitations. Your recall of these should be fast.

## Building a points bank by topic

A points bank is a compact list, per topic, of the issues, limitations and recommendations that tend to earn credit. For example, for a make-or-buy decision you might note: relevant costs only, spare capacity, quality control, supplier reliability, confidentiality and the strategic importance of the activity. For a variance question you might note: possible causes, whether the variance is controllable, interdependence with other variances and whether investigation is worth its cost.

Create the list from your own practice answers. After each essay, add any point you missed to the bank. Then use short retrieval drills: pick a topic, set a two-minute timer and write every point you can recall. This is the same principle as flashcards, applied to the structure of an answer.

### Turning a thin point into a full one

Most lost marks come from sentences that gesture at a point without making it. Compare two versions of the same idea from the Harbour Components example. Thin: *There could be problems with other customers.* Full: *Precedent: existing customers paying 70 may demand the 52 price if they learn of the deal, which would reduce contribution on current volume.* The second names the point, uses a figure from the scenario and states the consequence, which is what a marker can tick. Practise rewriting three thin sentences from each of your own answers in this way, and the habit transfers to the exam.

### Writing under pressure

- Use short sentences. They are quicker to write and easier to mark.
- Put the key word of each point at the start of the sentence, such as Capacity, Precedent or Quality, so the marker can find it.
- If you run short of time, write the recommendation first and return to fill gaps. A missing conclusion is harder to recover than a thin middle.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should a CMA essay answer be?

Long enough to make the expected points and no longer. Several short, specific points usually serve better than a long paragraph, and the time available will set your upper limit.

### Do I need to show calculations in an essay?

If the question gives figures, applying them usually strengthens your answer. Show the working briefly and say what it means for the decision.

### Can I earn marks without a perfect conclusion?

Partial credit is the point of a structured answer, but you should still make a clear recommendation, because it is usually one of the things a marker looks for.

### How do I improve if my essays feel vague?

Force specifics. Name the concept, cite a number or detail from the scenario and state the consequence. Then self-mark against a points list.
