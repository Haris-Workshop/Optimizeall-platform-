Almost nobody gets to study full time for a professional exam. The usual picture is a full working week, a family or a social life, and a goal that needs several months of steady effort. If you want to study for a certification while working, the challenge is not finding spare time: it is designing a routine that survives busy weeks, tired evenings and the occasional crisis.

This guide shows how to audit your time, build a 10 to 12 hour weekly template, plan rest and recovery, and have a useful conversation with your manager. It applies to any credential, from accounting and audit to finance, project management and nursing.

> Requirements, eligibility, exam formats, fees and rules are set by each credentialing body and change from time to time. Check the official body's current requirements for your exam before you commit to a timeline.

## Start with a time audit

Before you plan, measure. For one typical week, write down how you actually spend each block of time, in half-hour units if you can manage it. Include work, commuting, meals, sleep, exercise, family, chores and screen time. Most people are surprised by two things: how fragmented their free time is, and how much of it is lower value than they assumed.

Then sort the findings into three groups.

- **Fixed:** work, sleep, care responsibilities and commitments that cannot move.
- **Flexible:** social time, hobbies, errands and media, which can be shifted or trimmed.
- **Recoverable:** pockets such as a commute that could be used for retrieval practice, or a lunch break that can hold a short session.

The goal is not to eliminate everything enjoyable. It is to find a realistic number of study hours you can sustain, not a heroic one you cannot.

## How many hours do you actually have?

Be conservative. If the audit shows you can find about 10 to 12 hours a week with some effort, plan for 9 to 10 and keep the rest as slack. Three reliable hours beat six hours that exist only on paper. Then work out how many weeks your target exam needs by estimating the total hours from the official outline, rating each topic as new, rusty or strong, and dividing. If the answer looks too long, the solution is a longer runway, not a faster pace.

## A weekly template for 10 to 12 hours

The template below is **illustrative**. Adapt the days to your life.

| Day | Time | Session | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | 75 min | Learn | One new topic block, short notes |
| Tue | 60 min | Practice | Questions on Monday's topic, error log |
| Wed | 75 min | Learn | Second topic block |
| Thu | 60 min | Practice | Questions plus mixed review |
| Fri | 20 to 30 min | Light | Flashcards or retrieval drills only |
| Sat | 3 hrs | Deep | Timed mixed set, review of errors, one weak topic |
| Sun | 1.5 hrs | Plan | Weekly review, next week's plan, short teach-back |

Total: about 10 to 11 hours. Three principles hold the template together.

1. **Practice follows learning within a day or two,** because memory fades quickly without retrieval.
2. **Weekday sessions are short and specific.** Each has one aim, so you are never starting with a vague intention.
3. **One long session a week is protected.** It is the time for timed practice and honest review.

## Make small sessions count

Short sessions work if they are active. Instead of rereading, pull information from memory: write down the steps of a process, answer five questions or explain a concept aloud. The evidence for spacing and retrieval is summarised in [spaced repetition and active recall for professional exams](/blog/spaced-repetition-and-active-recall-for-exams). A useful rule is to start every session with two minutes of recalling what you studied last time, and end every session with one line on what to do next.

### Use the edges of the day

- **Commute or walking:** retrieval from flashcards or audio recall.
- **Lunch:** one short question set.
- **Evening:** reserve harder topics for when you are alert, not for your last tired half hour.

## Build rest and recovery into the plan

Burnout is a planning problem as much as a motivation problem. A schedule with no slack punishes you for being human. Build these in from the start.

- **A rest day each week.** One day with no study at all.
- **A lighter week every fourth or fifth week,** with fewer hours and more review, to absorb what you have learnt.
- **Sleep as a priority.** Memory consolidation depends on it, and a tired brain makes careless errors.
- **Buffer weeks** for work peaks, illness or travel.
- **A warning list.** Write down the signs that you are overreaching, such as persistent irritability, skipped meals or dread of sessions, and decide in advance what you will cut when two appear.

If you do fall behind, resist the urge to pile everything into one weekend. Remove low-value activities first, protect practice questions and move the date if you must.

## Talk to your manager

Many working candidates never mention their exam at work, then struggle during busy periods. A short, well-prepared conversation can help. Consider these points.

1. **Share the timeline,** including your exam dates and the preparation period.
2. **Name the benefit** to the team: the knowledge you build is relevant to your role.
3. **Make specific requests,** such as predictable hours in the final fortnight, a day off on exam day, or a clear process for last-minute demands.
4. **Ask about support** that may exist, such as study leave or sponsorship, without assuming it does.
5. **Offer reliability** in return: agree how you will cover critical work during peaks.

Every workplace differs, and no particular arrangement is certain, so treat the conversation as a negotiation and have a plan that works even if the answer is a polite no.

## Plan around your work calendar

Map your busy periods first. If your role has an annual peak, avoid placing an exam or the heaviest study phase there. Put lighter review activities in busy weeks and heavier learning in the quieter ones. When time is scarce, wasted practice is expensive. Keep an error log and review it weekly, using the approach in [how to review a mock exam](/blog/how-to-review-a-mock-exam). ## An illustrative month

To see how the pieces fit, picture a four-week cycle for someone aiming at about ten hours a week. This is **illustrative only**.

- **Week 1:** two new topic blocks, matching practice sessions, one timed set at the weekend.
- **Week 2:** the same pattern, with the first weekend session including a short review of last week's error log.
- **Week 3:** the same pattern, plus a cumulative mixed set covering every topic so far.
- **Week 4:** a lighter week of seven or eight hours, mostly review and retrieval, with a full rest day and an early night or two.

At the end of the month, spend twenty minutes comparing your plan with what actually happened. Which sessions were skipped, and why? If the same session is skipped every week, move it to a time when you are more likely to do it, rather than blaming willpower. Over several months, small adjustments like this matter far more than a perfect first plan.

### When life takes over

Some weeks you will study almost nothing. That is normal. Keep a minimum viable routine for those weeks: fifteen minutes of flashcards, a single short question set and a one-line note on what you will do next. It keeps the habit alive, protects what you have already learnt and makes restarting easy.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many hours a week should I study while working full time?

It depends on the exam and your background, but many working candidates find 8 to 12 hours sustainable. Estimate the total from the official outline and plan backwards, using hours you can reliably protect.

### Is it better to study every day or in long weekend blocks?

A mix works best. Short weekday sessions keep information fresh and reduce forgetting, and one longer weekend session suits timed practice and review.

### What should I do if I miss a week?

Do not try to double up. Use a buffer week if you have one, cut low-value tasks and keep practice questions. If you are repeatedly behind, extend the timeline.

### Should I tell my employer about my exam?

Often it helps, especially if you need predictable hours near the exam date. Prepare specific requests and a plan that works without special treatment.
