Plenty of well-prepared candidates lose marks not because they did not know the material but because they ran out of time. The last fifteen questions get a rushed guess, a simulation is left half-finished or an essay never gets its final paragraph. In long professional exams, pacing is a skill in its own right.

This guide sets out **exam time management** strategies that work across professional certifications — multiple-choice exams such as the PMP, CIA and CISA, exams with task-based simulations such as the CPA, and exams with written responses such as the CMA's essays. Exam formats change, so take the exact timings from your exam body's official handbook and appointment details, then apply the methods below.

## Step 1: Do the arithmetic before exam day

Start with the official numbers: total time, number of sections, number of questions or tasks per section, and any breaks. Then calculate:

- **Average time per question** — total section time divided by the number of questions.
- **Reserve** — a few minutes per section held back for flagged questions.
- **Working time per question** — (section time minus reserve) divided by questions.

For example, if a section allowed 60 minutes for 50 questions and you kept a 5-minute reserve, your working target would be 55 ÷ 50 = 1.1 minutes per question — about 66 seconds. The numbers for your exam will differ; the method is the same.

## Step 2: Set checkpoints

Per-question targets are hard to track. Checkpoints are easier: decide where you should be at each quarter of a section.

| Point in section | Question number | Minutes remaining (example) |
|---|---|---|
| Start | 1 | 60 |
| One quarter | 13 | about 46 |
| Halfway | 25 | about 32 |
| Three quarters | 38 | about 18 |
| End of first pass | 50 | 5 (reserve) |

Write your own checkpoints on your permitted materials if allowed, or memorise them. Glance at the clock only at checkpoints; constant clock-watching wastes time and feeds anxiety.

## Step 3: Use a flag-and-move rule

The biggest time sink is the one question you cannot decide. Agree a rule with yourself in advance:

1. Read the question stem's final sentence to see what is asked.
2. Eliminate clearly wrong options.
3. If you are still unsure after about one and a half times your target, choose the best remaining option, flag it and move on.

You will return to flagged questions with fresh eyes — and sometimes a later question will remind you of the principle you needed. Check your exam's rules on reviewing: some exams only allow review within a section, so return to flags before the section ends.

## Step 4: Read efficiently

Long scenario questions reward a consistent reading method:

- **Read the last line first.** Is it asking for the *first* action, the *best* response, the *most likely* cause?
- **Skim the scenario for context.** Which role are you in? What approach or standard applies? What has already happened?
- **Read the options.** Look for the one that answers the specific question asked, not just a true statement.

This method stops you reading a long scenario twice.

## Step 5: Manage different item types

### Task-based simulations

Simulations take longer than multiple-choice questions and carry significant weight. Strategies:

- Check the official guidance on how sections are timed, then plan a time budget per simulation.
- Skim all simulations in a testlet first if the format allows, and start with the one you find most approachable.
- Complete the parts you can do quickly before tackling the hardest elements; partial credit is often available.

Our guide to [CPA task-based simulations](/blog/cpa-task-based-simulations-guide) has more detail.

### Essays and written responses

Written responses need a plan:

- Spend a few minutes outlining before writing.
- Answer every requirement explicitly, using the question's wording as headings.
- Show calculations and reasoning clearly; examiners can only credit what they can see.
- Keep a time limit per requirement and move on when it is reached.

Our guide to [CMA essay questions technique](/blog/cma-essay-questions-technique) applies these ideas to the CMA.

### Calculations

Set up the formula before calculating, and write intermediate results if scratch materials are allowed. If a calculation is taking far longer than your target, flag it and return later.

## Step 6: Rehearse until it is automatic

Pacing strategies only work if you have practised them. In each full-length mock:

1. Use exactly the same checkpoints and flag rule you plan to use on exam day.
2. Note where you were at each checkpoint.
3. After the mock, look for patterns: are you slow at the start? Do you speed up and lose accuracy at the end?
4. Adjust your plan and test it again in the next mock.

Two or three rehearsals are usually enough to make pacing feel natural. Our guide to [PMP practice questions](/blog/pmp-practice-questions) explains how to use mocks for timing practice, and the [exam readiness checklist](/blog/exam-readiness-checklist) includes pacing as a readiness signal.

## Step 7: Protect your energy

Time management is also energy management. In long exams:

- use permitted breaks to stand, stretch and drink water;
- do a 60-second breathing reset when you notice tension — see our guide to [exam anxiety](/blog/exam-anxiety-strategies);
- avoid replaying hard questions from earlier sections.

## Common pacing mistakes

- **Starting slowly.** Many candidates spend too long on the first questions while they settle in, then rush the end. Your first checkpoint is there to catch this.
- **Re-reading every option several times.** Read the question's final sentence first so you know what you are looking for.
- **Fighting one question.** No single question is worth five minutes. Flag it and move on.
- **Leaving no reserve.** Without a few minutes at the end of a section, flagged questions never get a second look.
- **Changing pacing on the day.** Use exactly the plan you rehearsed. Exam day is not the time to experiment.

## A worked pacing example

Maya is preparing for a multiple-choice exam with three sections, each with its own time limit. In her first mock she reaches the end of section one with no time left and eight questions answered in a rush. Her checkpoint notes show she spent too long on the first ten questions, double-checking answers she already knew.

For the second mock she sets checkpoints at each quarter and adopts a flag-and-move rule. She finishes section one with four minutes in reserve, revisits five flagged questions and changes two answers after spotting principles in later questions. Her overall result improves, but more importantly, her accuracy on the last quarter of each section matches the rest.

## Pacing in shorter exams

Not every exam is a marathon. Shorter, scenario-based exams — such as PCI AI's 90-minute PCL-AI, PFL-AI and PML-AI exams — still benefit from the same method: work out a per-question target from the official question count and time, set two or three checkpoints and use a flag-and-move rule. Short exams leave less room to recover from a slow start, so the first checkpoint matters even more.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should I spend on each exam question?

Divide each section's time, less a small reserve, by the number of questions. Use checkpoints rather than timing every question individually.

### Should I answer every question even if unsure?

On exams that do not penalise wrong answers, a blank answer cannot earn credit. Check your exam's scoring rules in the official handbook; if there is no penalty, always choose your best remaining option.

### Is it better to go back and change answers?

Change an answer when you find a clear reason, such as a misread question or a principle you remember later. Avoid changing answers based on a vague feeling.

### How do I stop rushing at the end?

Use checkpoints to catch slow starts early, adopt a flag-and-move rule and rehearse both in full-length mocks.

### Does pacing work the same on adaptive exams?

The principles are similar, but adaptive exams such as the NCLEX can vary in length. Read the official candidate information carefully, focus on steady, careful reasoning on every item and avoid rushing because questions feel difficult.
