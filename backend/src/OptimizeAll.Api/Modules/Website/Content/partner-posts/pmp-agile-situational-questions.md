Candidates who come from predictive, plan-driven backgrounds often dread the agile side of the PMP exam. They know the vocabulary of sprints and backlogs, yet still miss questions, because PMP agile questions are not vocabulary tests. They are situational: a team is in a certain context, something has gone wrong or a decision is needed, and four plausible options follow. Your job is to pick the response that a thoughtful project leader would give in that context.

This article offers a reasoning method for those questions, with worked examples, elimination rules and a link to the PMBOK 7 principles.

> The exam content outline, the balance of predictive, agile and hybrid content, question formats, eligibility and fees are set by the Project Management Institute (PMI) and may change. Check the official body's current requirements and the current Examination Content Outline before you plan your study.

## Why situational questions feel hard

Three features make them tricky.

- **More than one option looks reasonable.** You are choosing the best option, not the only correct one.
- **The context is a clue.** An agile team, a hybrid programme and a regulated predictive project call for different responses to the same symptom.
- **Your workplace habits can mislead.** If your organisation tends to escalate early or to fix problems by decree, you may pick answers that the exam treats as weaker.

## A five-step reasoning method

1. **Name the context.** Is the delivery approach predictive, agile or hybrid? Is the issue about people, process or the business environment?
2. **Name the problem in one sentence.** What is actually going wrong, as opposed to the symptom?
3. **Recall the principle at stake.** Is it collaboration, value delivery, stakeholder engagement, adaptability, leadership or risk?
4. **Eliminate options that break the principle.** Remove those that skip analysis, bypass the team, escalate prematurely or ignore the context.
5. **Choose the action that fits the context, closes the gap and respects roles.** Prefer the response that is proportionate and keeps the team and stakeholders involved.

## Link to the PMBOK 7 principles

PMBOK 7 presents twelve principles of project management: stewardship, team, stakeholders, value, systems thinking, leadership, tailoring, quality, complexity, risk, adaptability and resiliency, and change. They are not an agile-only list, but they describe the mindset that situational answers tend to reflect: collaborative, value-focused, adaptive and responsible. When two options seem equal, ask which one better reflects a principle that fits the context. Our guide to [PMP exam preparation with PMBOK 7](/blog/pmp-exam-prep-pmbok-7) covers the principles and performance domains in more depth.

## Worked example 1: a team conflict

The scenario is **fictional and illustrative**.

*An agile team is two sprints into a product build. Two developers disagree about a design approach, and the disagreement is slowing progress. The project manager is a servant leader for the team. What should the project manager do first?*

- A. Decide which design to use and inform the team.
- B. Escalate the disagreement to the sponsor.
- C. Facilitate a discussion so that the developers can resolve it, helping them focus on the shared goal.
- D. Ask the team to postpone the design decision until the next retrospective.

Reasoning: the context is agile, the problem is a team conflict and the principle is leadership and team empowerment. A takes the decision away from a self-organising team. B escalates before the team has tried to resolve it. D delays a blocker. **C is best**: it supports the team to resolve its own issue.

## Worked example 2: a changing requirement

*Late in a hybrid project, a key stakeholder asks for a new feature. The core build follows a predictive plan, while the user interface is developed iteratively.*

- A. Reject the request because the scope is baselined.
- B. Add the feature to the iterative backlog straight away.
- C. Assess the impact of the request on scope, schedule, cost and risk, and process it through the agreed change approach, using the backlog for the iterative part if approved.
- D. Tell the stakeholder that changes are not permitted after a baseline.

Reasoning: the context is hybrid, so both mechanisms exist. A and D are rigid and ignore value. B skips the change control that governs the predictive part. **C is best** because it analyses before acting and respects both approaches.

## Worked example 3: a stakeholder who wants status

*A product owner says the team's daily meeting is a waste of time and asks to replace it with a weekly written report. The team uses a lightweight agile approach.*

The tempting answers are to comply or to refuse. The better answer is to explore the underlying need, which might be visibility, and discuss whether other transparency practices such as a visible task board or a review meeting could meet it without losing the team's coordination. The principle here is stakeholder engagement and tailoring, and the method is to seek understanding before choosing.

## Elimination patterns that work

- **Skip options that act without analysis,** such as changing the plan before understanding the cause.
- **Skip options that bypass the team** when the team is capable of solving the problem.
- **Be wary of immediate escalation.** Escalation is appropriate for issues beyond your authority or when other routes have failed, not as a reflex.
- **Be wary of rigid process answers** in a context that values adaptation, and of chaotic answers in a context that demands governance.
- **Prefer options that restore value and transparency,** such as clarifying the goal, refining the backlog, or involving the right stakeholders.

## Vocabulary to know cold

You do not need every term, but be solid on the ones that appear in scenarios: backlog, iteration or sprint, retrospective, definition of done, product owner, servant leadership, velocity as a planning aid rather than a performance target, and the idea of delivering increments of value. For hybrid scenarios, be comfortable describing which parts of a project are plan-driven and which are adaptive, as discussed in [agile versus hybrid project delivery](/blog/agile-vs-hybrid-project-delivery-with-ai).

## Practice routine

1. **Do ten situational questions at a time,** and write your context, problem and principle before choosing.
2. **Review wrong answers by cause:** did you misjudge the context, miss the principle or fall for an attractive but premature action? The [mock exam review method](/blog/how-to-review-a-mock-exam) provides categories.
3. **Rewrite two or three options per session** to say why they fail.
4. **Mix predictive, agile and hybrid questions,** since the real exam does.

For the broader technique of parsing and eliminating, see [scenario-based exam questions: parse, decide, eliminate](/blog/scenario-based-exam-questions-technique). ## Worked example 4: a risk that has appeared mid-iteration

*During a sprint, a developer reports that a third-party component the team planned to use may not meet performance needs. The team has a working alternative that would take two extra days. The sprint goal is still achievable if the team swaps components. What should the project manager do?*

- A. Wait for the next sprint review to raise it.
- B. Ask the team to evaluate the options, make the trade-off visible to the product owner and let the team decide how to protect the sprint goal.
- C. Replace the component personally and update the plan.
- D. Log the risk and take no further action until it becomes an issue.

The context is agile, the problem is an emerging risk and the principles in play are risk, transparency and team ownership. A delays communication. C removes the team's ownership. D is passive when action is cheap. **B is best**: it surfaces the trade-off to the person who owns value and keeps the decision with the team.

### What the four examples have in common

In every case, the winning option involves understanding before acting, engaging the right people and choosing a proportionate response. The losing options fall into a small number of families: the unilateral decision, the premature escalation, the passive delay and the rigid refusal. If you can name which family an option belongs to, elimination becomes much faster. Build a personal list of these families and tag your practice errors with them.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need real agile experience to answer PMP agile questions?

No, but you need the mindset. Study what agile roles and practices aim to achieve, then practise applying that logic to scenarios.

### Should I always choose the collaborative answer?

Usually collaboration is favoured, but not blindly. If a question involves authority, compliance or a safety issue, the best answer may involve escalation or formal change control.

### How do hybrid questions differ from agile ones?

Hybrid questions mix plan-driven and adaptive elements, so look for the answer that respects both, such as using a formal change process for baselined work and a backlog for iterative work.
