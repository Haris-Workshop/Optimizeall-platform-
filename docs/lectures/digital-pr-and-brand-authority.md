Scene 1 — What makes a story newsworthy

```text
Here are two headlines. Headline one: local company releases its annual report. Headline two: half of small business websites in the UAE can't be found for their own brand name, study finds. Which one would you click? Exactly. And which one would a journalist run? The same one. That's the whole game of digital PR in a sentence: give journalists stories their readers genuinely want. In this lecture, you'll learn the news values editors use, how to find a hook, the difference between reactive and proactive PR, which campaign formats tend to work, and how to score ideas so brand relevance never gets lost.
```

Scene 2 — Why it matters

```text
Why does newsworthiness matter so much? Because digital PR earns coverage, links, mentions and brand authority by serving a newsroom, not a marketing team. Journalists work to deadlines, under editors, for readers who can scroll away in a second. And here's the bigger reason. Coverage in reputable publications is how independent sources come to describe your brand. Those descriptions shape how search engines and AI answer engines understand what you're credible on. Think of it like references on a job application. Your own CV says you're great. References from respected people are what the hiring manager actually believes.
```

Scene 3 — The news values

```text
Let's go through the news values. Journalism schools teach variations of the same list. Timeliness: why now? Proximity: why does this matter to our readers, in this city or country? Impact: how many people does it affect, and how much? Novelty: is this new or surprising? Conflict or tension: is there a debate or a contrast? Human interest: is there a person or an emotion at the heart of it? Prominence: does it involve a well-known person, place or brand? And usefulness: will readers act on it? A story needs at least two of these to have a real chance.
```

Scene 4 — Hooks: why now?

```text
A hook is the reason a journalist would publish now. Reliable hooks include calendar moments, like Ramadan, Eid, Diwali, Christmas, Black Friday, back to school, tax deadlines, budget announcements, and national days such as UAE National Day, Saudi National Day and Pakistan's Independence Day. News events, like regulation changes and platform updates. Trends, like rising search interest. Anniversaries. And data releases, your own or new analysis of public data. Here's the key idea. Your expertise stays the same all year. The hook is what makes it relevant this week.
```

Scene 5 — Reactive vs proactive

```text
Two modes of PR. Reactive PR, sometimes called newsjacking, means responding quickly to breaking news with expert comment or rapid data. Speed matters, hours not days, so you need experts on standby and pre-approved sign-off. Proactive PR means planned campaigns, with original data, creative assets or stories, launched on a date you choose. They have a longer shelf life and earn more links, but take more effort. Healthy programs run both. Reactive work keeps relationships warm between big moments. Proactive campaigns deliver the big moments.
```

Scene 6 — Formats that work

```text
Formats that tend to work. Data studies and indexes, like the most affordable cities for freelancers, built from public data plus your analysis. Surveys of a defined group, with rigorous methodology. Proprietary data insights, anonymized and aggregated from your platform, with privacy safeguards. Tools and interactive experiences, like calculators, quizzes and maps that journalists can reference. Expert guides tied to a moment, like how to protect your home during a sandstorm, from a property maintenance firm. And creative visual campaigns, like reimagined maps and visualizations. Each one gives journalists something they can't easily produce themselves.
```

Scene 7 — The 'so what?' test

```text
Now the so what test. For every idea, write the headline a journalist might actually use. Then ask: would I click this if I weren't in this industry? If the headline only excites your marketing team, like company X launches new feature, it isn't news for consumer or business media, though it might suit trade press, which does cover launches and partnerships in its sector. And one rule that never bends. Only publish claims your data genuinely supports. Exaggerated findings damage your reputation with journalists permanently, and can breach advertising and consumer protection rules.
```

Scene 8 — Worked example 1: relevance beats reach

```text
Worked example one, simple. A tire retailer runs a celebrity baby names study. It wins national coverage. Everyone celebrates. But what did it build? Very little. The links and mentions have nothing to do with tires, safety or driving, so they do almost nothing for the brand's authority on those topics. Compare that with a study of which cities have the most pothole complaints per mile of road, using public council data. Less glamorous, maybe. But it's relevant, credible, and it gets the brand cited in exactly the conversations where it wants to be trusted.
```

Scene 9 — Worked example 2: Dubai agency scoring (illustrative)

```text
Worked example two, a business scenario with illustrative details. A small SEO agency in Dubai wants coverage. The team brainstorms ten headlines and scores each one in a sheet: seven news values, each scored zero to two, plus brand relevance and credibility, each zero to three. A formula multiplies the news score by relevance plus credibility, and gives zero if either is zero. The winner: how many UAE small businesses can't be found for their own brand name, based on a genuine sample of searches. It scores well on proximity, impact and novelty, and it's squarely in the agency's expertise. A fun idea about pet names scores zero for relevance and is dropped.
```

Scene 10 — Watch me do it: score and pressure-test

```text
Watch me do it. I open the scoring sheet and paste in ten draft headlines. For each, I score timeliness, proximity, impact, novelty, conflict, human interest and usefulness. Zero, one or two. Then brand relevance and credibility, zero to three. The total formula does the rest. Next, I paste the top five into an AI assistant and ask it to play a skeptical editor at a UAE business site: which news values does each have, what's the first question you'd ask, and would you run it? No invented data allowed. Its toughest question, how big is your sample, goes straight into my methodology plan.
```

Scene 11 — Common mistakes and measures

```text
Common mistakes. Pitching company news as if it were consumer news. Ignoring timing, because the best idea launched in a crowded news week sinks. Stretching data to fit a headline. Running campaigns with no connection to the brand's expertise. And treating AI-generated angles as if they were findings. How do you measure success? For each idea, track whether it passed scoring, whether it launched, coverage by tier, and the links and mentions it earned. Over time, your scoring sheet learns which news values and formats consistently win in your niche.
```

Scene 12 — Recap and try this now

```text
Let's recap. Newsworthy stories combine news values: timeliness, proximity, impact, novelty, conflict, human interest, prominence and usefulness. A hook explains why now. Run reactive and proactive PR together. Choose formats journalists can't easily create themselves. Apply the so what test, and only publish what your data supports. And always aim for the overlap of journalist interest, audience interest and brand credibility. Try this now. Brainstorm ten campaign headlines for a brand you know, score each one in the sheet, and pick the two strongest to take into the next lesson.
```

Scene 1 — From ideation to launch

```text

```

Scene 2 — Why a process?

```text
Why a process? Because successful digital PR teams don't rely on inspiration. They run a funnel that turns many ideas into a few strong, validated campaigns. Think of it like a film studio. Hundreds of scripts come in. A few get developed. Fewer get made. And each one goes through the same stages: script, casting, filming, editing, release. Nobody starts filming on day one. ```

Scene 3 — Stages 1 and 2

```text

```text
A validation trick. Search the news for similar stories from the last two years. If similar campaigns got coverage, the topic has appetite. ```text

Scene 1 — Press releases vs pitches

```text
Here's a common misunderstanding that costs companies real money. We'll put out a press release on a big wire service, and that'll build links. It won't, at least not the way people hope. Syndicated release pages are typically nofollowed or not indexed, and Google has long treated links in widely distributed releases as not passing ranking credit. So what is a press release for? And when should you write a personal pitch instead? In this lecture, you'll learn the difference, how to write both well, how embargoes and exclusives work, and how to turn one announcement into a release plus tailored pitches.
```

Scene 2 — Two tools, two jobs

```text
Why does the distinction matter? Because they're two different tools for two different jobs. A press release is a formal, complete announcement in news style, for many outlets, newswires and your newsroom page. A pitch is a short, personal email proposing a story angle to one journalist or a small segment. Think of it like a public notice on a town hall board versus a personal invitation. The notice is official, complete and for everyone. The invitation is short, personal, and says why this particular person should come. Most data stories and expert commentary need invitations.
```

Scene 3 — When a release fits

```text
When is a press release the right choice? Funding rounds, acquisitions, leadership appointments and financial results. Official responses where exact wording matters, like legal or crisis statements. Regulated sectors that need a public, formal record. And major launches where you want a canonical, quotable source on your newsroom page. What's a release worth in search terms? Mostly visibility. Journalists and the public may see it and write original coverage, and that original coverage is what earns links and mentions. Treat the wire as a megaphone, not a link-building machine.
```

Scene 4 — Writing a release

```text
Writing a release. Start with the dateline: city, country and date, and if embargoed, the exact time and time zone. A clear, factual headline, around twelve words or fewer. A subheadline adding the key detail. A lead paragraph covering who, what, when, where and why in one or two sentences. Then the most important supporting detail. A quote from a named spokesperson that adds opinion and meaning, not repetition. Further detail, like availability or methodology. An about section of fifty to eighty words. And media contact details. Keep it to one page, and skip superlatives like world-leading unless you can prove them.
```

Scene 5 — Writing a pitch

```text
Writing a pitch. The subject line is the headline you imagine them running. Then a one-line hook: why this matters to their readers now. Three bullet findings or key facts, specific and verifiable. What you can offer: full data, an expert interview, images, local cuts, maybe an exclusive. One link to the landing page, no attachments. And a signature with a direct phone number, because journalists on deadline call. Keep it under about a hundred and fifty words. And personalize it. A sentence showing you've read their recent coverage is worth more than any clever phrasing.
```

Scene 6 — Embargoes, exclusives, newsroom

```text
Embargoes and exclusives. An embargo lets journalists prepare a story before a set publication time. Use them for significant data or news, state the time zone clearly, send only to journalists you trust, and never punish a journalist for your own unclear terms. An exclusive gives one outlet a reason to prioritize your story. Be precise about what exclusive means: first to publish, or the only outlet, and for how long. After it runs, pitch wider media with a fresh angle. And keep a newsroom page on your site with releases, a media kit, logos, spokesperson bios and contact details.
```

```text

Full data, methodology and city charts at one link. And the head of research available for interview today, in Arabic or English. It's short, personal and useful. Most importantly, every finding in it matches the actual analysis.
```

Scene 8 — Worked example 2: funding round (illustrative)

```text
Worked example two, a business scenario with illustrative details. A logistics software company in Riyadh closes a funding round. They publish a formal release on their newsroom page and a wire service, because exact wording and a public record matter. Then they write three separate pitches from one fact sheet. A trade pitch on what the funding means for last-mile delivery in the region. A Riyadh business reporter pitch on local jobs and expansion. ```

```text

Why it matters to customers, the industry and a local community. Assets and the contact. Scene 10 — One announcement, several stories

```text
Let's talk about segments, because one announcement is rarely one story. National consumer desks want impact on everyday life. Business desks want numbers and what they signal about the market. Regional outlets want the local angle: jobs, customers, a named city. And trade publications want what it means for their industry, including the operational detail everyone else ignores. So write one pitch per segment, each with its own subject line and first sentence. Time your sends for each segment's local morning, and remember that working weeks differ between the Gulf, the UK and the US. Then track which segment responds, so the next campaign starts smarter.
```

Scene 11 — Common mistakes and measures

```text

Attachments instead of a link. And sending AI-written pitches unedited. Journalists spot generic drafts quickly, and many simply ignore them. How do you measure success? Track replies and coverage per segment, and the ratio of pitches sent to pieces of coverage. When that ratio falls, it usually means better targeting, not just better writing.
```

```text

A calm, helpful reply to a bad review can build more trust than a dozen five-star ratings.

Scene 2 — What E-E-A-T is

```text
E-E-A-T stands for experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trust. It comes from Google's search quality rater guidelines, used by human raters who evaluate search results to help Google test and improve its systems. Key points. E-E-A-T is a framework for assessing quality, not a single ranking factor or score. Google has said its systems use a mix of signals that align with what raters would consider E-E-A-T. Trust is the most important element, and experience, expertise and authoritativeness support it. Think of it like a doctor's office. The certificates on the wall matter. Scene 3 — YMYL + the four components

```text
The bar is highest for YMYL topics: your money or your life. That means health, finance, safety, legal and civic information, where poor content could harm people. Now the four components. Experience: has the creator actually used, done or lived this? First-hand photos, original test results, case studies. Expertise: does the creator have the knowledge or skill? Qualifications, professional role, track record. Authoritativeness: is the creator or site a go-to source? Citations by others, reputable coverage, recognition. And trust: is the page accurate, honest, safe and reliable? Clear sourcing, transparency about who's behind it, secure checkout, policies and corrections.

```text

Using AI to help create content is not against Google's guidelines. Using automation to mass-produce pages primarily to manipulate rankings is, and Google calls that scaled content abuse.
