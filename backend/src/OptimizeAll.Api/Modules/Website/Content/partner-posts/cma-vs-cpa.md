Accounting and finance professionals often weigh two well-known credentials: the **CMA** (Certified Management Accountant), awarded by the Institute of Management Accountants (IMA), and the **CPA** (Certified Public Accountant), the US licence regulated by state boards of accountancy with the Uniform CPA Examination developed by the AICPA. Both are respected, both are demanding, and they lead in somewhat different directions.

This guide compares **CMA vs CPA** on focus, exam structure, licensing, career paths and preparation, and offers a framework for deciding. Requirements change and, for the CPA, vary by jurisdiction, so always check the official requirements of IMA, the AICPA and NASBA, and the specific state board where you intend to be licensed.

## The core difference in one sentence

The **CPA** is a licence centred on external reporting, audit, tax and regulation, with legal rights in areas such as signing audit opinions in the US. The **CMA** is a professional certification centred on management accounting and financial management inside organisations: planning, analysis, performance, cost management and strategic decision support.

## Side-by-side comparison

| | CMA | CPA (US) |
|---|---|---|
| Awarding or regulating body | IMA | State boards of accountancy; exam by the AICPA, with NASBA administering key processes |
| Nature | Global professional certification | Licence to practise public accounting in a US jurisdiction |
| Focus | Planning, budgeting, performance management, cost management, analytics, corporate finance, decision analysis, risk, ethics | Audit and attestation, financial reporting, taxation and regulation, plus a chosen discipline |
| Exam structure | Two parts, each combining multiple-choice questions and essay scenarios | Three core sections plus one discipline section |
| Typical eligibility themes | Degree (or approved alternative), relevant professional experience, IMA membership | Education requirements (credit hours or alternative pathways), experience, often an ethics exam; varies by state |
| Typical career direction | Corporate finance, FP&A, controllership, cost and management accounting, CFO track in industry | Public accounting, audit, tax, financial reporting, advisory, and many industry finance roles |

Use this as orientation only. The exam content, formats and eligibility rules are defined in the official handbooks.

## Exam structure in more detail

### CMA

The CMA exam has two parts:

- **Part 1 — Financial Planning, Performance, and Analytics.**
- **Part 2 — Strategic Financial Management.**

Each part combines multiple-choice questions with essay scenarios, so preparation must cover written explanation as well as calculation. Our guide to [CMA essay questions](/blog/cma-essay-questions-technique) explains how to structure answers, and our [CMA exam study plan](/blog/cma-exam-study-plan) offers a 12-week schedule per part.

### CPA

Under the current licensure model, candidates sit three **core** sections — Auditing and Attestation (AUD), Financial Accounting and Reporting (FAR), and Taxation and Regulation (REG) — and choose one **discipline** section: Business Analysis and Reporting (BAR), Information Systems and Controls (ISC) or Tax Compliance and Planning (TCP). Sections combine multiple-choice questions with task-based simulations. Our guides to [CPA exam sections](/blog/cpa-exam-sections-explained) and [how to pass the CPA exam](/blog/how-to-pass-the-cpa-exam) go deeper.

## Licensing and maintenance

**CPA.** Passing the exam is one step towards a licence. State boards also set education and experience requirements, and many require an ethics exam. Several states have introduced alternative pathways to licensure in recent years, so the rules depend heavily on where you plan to be licensed. Once licensed, CPAs complete continuing professional education under their state's rules.

**CMA.** After passing both parts and meeting the education and experience requirements, CMAs maintain the credential through continuing professional education and active IMA membership, following IMA's current requirements.

## Career paths: where each tends to lead

### The CMA tends to suit

- financial planning and analysis (FP&A) professionals;
- cost accountants, management accountants and controllers in industry;
- business partners who support operations with analysis and decision support;
- finance professionals outside the US who want an internationally portable management accounting credential.

### The CPA tends to suit

- auditors and assurance professionals;
- tax professionals;
- financial reporting specialists;
- anyone who needs the legal rights of a licensed CPA in the US;
- professionals who want the broadest recognition in US public accounting.

Many finance leaders in industry hold both: the CPA for technical reporting depth and the CMA for management and strategic finance.

## A decision framework

Answer these five questions honestly:

1. **Where do you want to work in five years?** Public accounting, audit or tax points towards the CPA. Corporate finance and management accounting point towards the CMA.
2. **Do you need a licence?** If your target role requires a licensed CPA, the decision is made.
3. **Can you meet the eligibility rules?** CPA education requirements can be significant and vary by state; check your route. CMA requirements differ; check IMA's.
4. **How much study time can you sustain?** The CPA involves four separate sections; the CMA involves two parts with essays. Both demand months of steady work.
5. **What do employers in your market ask for?** Read job adverts for your target roles; the credentials named are the market's answer.

## Doing both

Some professionals take one and later the other. Common sequences:

- **CPA first, CMA later** — typical for people who start in public accounting and move into industry.
- **CMA first, CPA later (or not at all)** — typical for people already in industry who do not need a licence but may later want the CPA's breadth.

Overlap exists in financial reporting, cost concepts and ethics, which can make the second credential somewhat easier. Plan each separately with its own blueprint.

## Two worked decisions

**Aisha** works in an audit firm in the US and wants to become an audit manager. She needs the legal rights that come with a CPA licence, and her firm supports candidates through the exam. The decision is straightforward: the CPA first, planning her four sections around the firm's busy season. She may never need the CMA, though it could help if she later moves into corporate finance.

**Tomás** is a financial analyst at a manufacturing company outside the US. He builds budgets, analyses product margins and supports investment decisions. He has no plans to work in public accounting. The CMA aligns closely with his daily work, is recognised internationally and has two parts rather than four. He chooses the CMA first and will revisit the question of a second credential once he has completed it.

The pattern is simple: let the work you want to do decide, then check eligibility and time.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the CMA easier than the CPA?

They test different things. The CPA has more sections and a strong technical focus on audit, reporting and tax; the CMA has fewer parts but requires essay-based explanation of management accounting and finance. Difficulty depends on your background.

### Can non-US professionals take the CPA?

Many international candidates sit the CPA exam, subject to the eligibility rules of the jurisdiction they apply through. Check NASBA's and the relevant state board's current rules.

### Do I need work experience before taking either exam?

Generally, you can sit the exams before completing the experience requirement, but you must meet it before receiving the credential or licence. Check IMA's and your state board's rules.

### Which pays better, CMA or CPA?

Outcomes depend on role, industry, location and experience rather than the credential alone. Focus on the credential that matches the work you want to do.
