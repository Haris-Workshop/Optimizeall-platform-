Whether you are preparing for the PMP, CPA, CMA, CIA, CISA, CFA, NCLEX or a project controls credential, the shape of good preparation is remarkably similar. You need to know what the exam covers, where your gaps are, how much time you really have and how you will check progress along the way.

This **certification study plan** template takes you through those decisions in about an hour. At the end, you will have a week-by-week plan, practice targets, a mock exam schedule and a review loop. Use it with the official exam blueprint or content outline for your credential — that document, not any study guide, defines what you must know.

## Step 1: Collect the official facts (10 minutes)

From the exam body's official website and candidate handbook, note:

- the current exam blueprint or content outline, with domains and weights;
- the exam format: number of questions or tasks, time, breaks, question types;
- eligibility rules and any application steps or deadlines;
- the testing window, scheduling rules and rescheduling deadlines;
- any official sample questions or practice materials.

Write these on one page. If you cannot find something, that is your first research task. Exam bodies update their rules, so check the official requirements rather than relying on forum posts or older guides.

## Step 2: Run a gap analysis (15 minutes)

List every domain or major topic from the blueprint in a table. For each, rate yourself honestly:

| Domain | Weight | Rating (1–3) | Evidence | Priority |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Domain A | 25% | 2 | Use at work, unsure of terminology | Medium |
| Domain B | 30% | 1 | New to me | High |
| Domain C | 20% | 3 | Daily work, strong | Low |
| Domain D | 25% | 1 | Studied years ago | High |

Ratings:

- **1** — new to me or forgotten;
- **2** — familiar but cannot apply confidently;
- **3** — apply at work confidently.

Better still, take a short diagnostic quiz and use the results as evidence. Priority combines weight and weakness: a heavily weighted domain rated 1 comes first.

## Step 3: Count your real hours (5 minutes)

Look at a typical week and write down the time you can protect — not the time you hope to find. For many working professionals, that is somewhere between five and ten hours a week. Multiply by the number of weeks until your target date and subtract a buffer of about 15% for illness, work peaks and family events.

If the total looks too small for the gap you found in step 2, move the exam date now rather than discovering it in week six. Our guide to [studying for a certification while working full time](/blog/study-for-certification-while-working-full-time) has practical ways to protect time.

## Step 4: Build the weekly blocks (15 minutes)

Divide the plan into four phases:

| Phase | Share of time | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Foundation | About 15% | Read the blueprint, diagnostic, set up error log and flashcards |
| Learning cycles | About 50% | Topic by topic: learn, then practise questions immediately |
| Repair | About 20% | Mixed practice and first mocks; target weakest areas |
| Consolidation | About 15% | Final mocks, light review, exam logistics |

Assign domains to learning-cycle weeks in priority order, keeping one shorter session each week for spaced review of earlier topics. A sample week:

- two learning sessions on new material;
- two practice-question sessions with full review;
- one short spaced-review session (flashcards and error log);
- one longer weekend session of mixed questions under time pressure.

For a fully worked example, see our [8-week PMP study plan](/blog/pmp-study-plan), which applies this template to one exam.

## Step 5: Set practice and mock targets (5 minutes)

Decide:

- **Practice questions per week** — enough to cover each topic, with time to review every question. Quality of review matters more than volume.
- **Number of full-length mocks** — usually two to four, starting around the midpoint.
- **Mock dates** — put them in your calendar now, at the same time of day as your exam.

Choose a question bank aligned to the current blueprint with clear explanations; our guide on [how to choose an exam question bank](/blog/how-to-choose-an-exam-question-bank) lists what to check.

## Step 6: Design your review loop (5 minutes)

Every week, spend 20 minutes reviewing progress:

1. **What did I plan, and what did I do?**
2. **What do my practice results say about each domain?**
3. **What are the top three recurring mistakes in my error log?**
4. **What will I change next week?**

After each mock, do a deeper review. Our guide on [how to review a mock exam](/blog/how-to-review-a-mock-exam) gives a step-by-step method.

## Step 7: Define "ready" in advance (5 minutes)

Write down what readiness will look like before you start: for example, "consistent mock results that I am comfortable with across three mocks on unseen questions, no domain consistently weak, and finishing on time." Deciding in advance protects you from anxiety-driven judgements late in the plan. Our [exam readiness checklist](/blog/exam-readiness-checklist) and printable [exam readiness scorecard (PDF)](/downloads/exam-readiness-scorecard.pdf) give you a ready-made version.

## A worked example: one exam part in ten weeks

Sam is preparing for CIA Part 1 while working full time. Her official facts page lists the domains and weights from the IIA's current syllabus. Her diagnostic shows she is strong on governance and control from her work, weaker on ethics and professionalism questions, and weakest on fraud risks.

She can protect about eight hours a week. Over ten weeks that is 80 hours; after a 15% buffer she plans for 68. Her phases:

- **Week 1 (foundation):** read the syllabus, take the diagnostic, set up the error log and flashcards.
- **Weeks 2–6 (learning cycles):** fraud risks and ethics first because they are weak, then the remaining domains in order of weight; topic questions after every learning session.
- **Weeks 7–8 (repair):** first full mock at the end of week 7; targeted work on the two weakest domains in week 8; second mock at the end of week 8.
- **Weeks 9–10 (consolidation):** mixed sets, a final mock early in week 9, light review and logistics in week 10.

Her definition of ready: consistent mock results across three unseen mocks, no domain consistently weak, and finishing each mock with time to spare. When she plans Part 2, she will reuse the same template with a fresh gap analysis. ## Common planning mistakes

- **Planning around hope.** Use the hours you can actually protect.
- **Reading first, practising later.** Practise from week two.
- **Equal time for every topic.** Weight time by blueprint weight and weakness.
- **No buffer.** Something will go wrong; plan for it.
- **Booking the exam before knowing the gap.** Run the diagnostic first, then book.

## Frequently asked questions

### How far in advance should I start studying for a certification?

It depends on the exam and your starting point. Many working professionals plan two to four months for a single exam or exam part. Your gap analysis and available hours give the best answer.

### Should I study one domain at a time or mix them?

Learn one topic at a time in the early weeks, but start mixing topics in practice sets from the midpoint. Exams mix topics, and mixed practice improves your ability to recognise what each question is about.

### How do I stay on track when work gets busy?

Keep a minimum routine — for example, 20 minutes of practice questions a day — during busy weeks, and use your plan's buffer rather than trying to double up later.

### Is it better to use one resource or several?

One main question bank and one main content resource, both aligned to the current blueprint, are usually enough. Too many resources dilute your time.
