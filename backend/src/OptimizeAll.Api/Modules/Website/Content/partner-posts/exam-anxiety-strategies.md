Professional exams carry real stakes: fees, career plans, time taken from family and work, and sometimes the expectations of an employer. It is no surprise that many capable candidates feel anxious. A little arousal is helpful — it sharpens focus. Too much can crowd out working memory, slow reading and make familiar material feel strange.

The good news is that **exam anxiety** responds well to preparation and to a few simple techniques with research behind them. This guide sets out strategies for the weeks before, the day itself and the moments in the exam when nerves spike. It is general guidance, not medical advice: if anxiety is severe, persistent or affecting your daily life, speak to a doctor or a qualified mental health professional.

## Why anxiety hurts performance

Professional exams rely heavily on working memory: holding a scenario in mind, comparing options and applying a principle. Worry competes for the same limited capacity. That is why anxious candidates often report reading a question three times without taking it in, or "blanking" on a topic they know well. The aim is not to eliminate nerves completely but to keep them from taking over that capacity.

## In the weeks before: build confidence from evidence

### Practise retrieval, not re-reading

Retrieval practice — testing yourself rather than re-reading notes — is one of the best-supported learning techniques. Research also suggests that knowledge learned through retrieval practice holds up better under stress than knowledge learned by restudying. Every practice question you answer and review is both learning and anxiety protection. Our guide to [spaced repetition and active recall](/blog/spaced-repetition-and-active-recall-for-exams) explains how to build it into your routine.

### Rehearse the exam experience

Uncertainty feeds anxiety. Remove as much as possible by rehearsing:

- full-length mocks at the same time of day as your real exam;
- the same pacing checkpoints you will use on the day;
- the same environment, if you are testing online;
- the same break routine.

By exam day, the experience should feel familiar. Our guide to [exam time management](/blog/exam-time-management-strategies) covers pacing in detail.

### Judge readiness with data, not feelings

Anxiety distorts self-assessment. Instead of asking "do I feel ready?", look at evidence: consistent mock results, coverage of every domain, and an error log that shows fewer repeated mistakes. Our [exam readiness checklist](/blog/exam-readiness-checklist) gives you a structured way to decide.

### Protect the basics

Sleep, regular exercise and normal meals sound obvious, but they are the first things to slip during intense study. Sleep in particular supports memory consolidation; regular late-night cramming is usually a poor trade.

## The day before and the morning of the exam

- **Stop heavy study.** A short, light review of key principles is fine; a full mock is not.
- **Prepare logistics early.** ID, route or room set-up, system checks. Our [PMP exam day checklist](/blog/pmp-exam-day-checklist) applies to most computer-based exams.
- **Plan your morning.** Decide when you will wake, eat and leave or log in. Fewer decisions mean less worry.

### Write your worries down

One simple technique with research support is expressive writing: spending about ten minutes writing honestly about your worries shortly before an exam. The idea is that putting worries on paper reduces how much they intrude on working memory during the test. It costs nothing and takes ten minutes; try it before a mock first so it feels natural.

### Reframe the feeling

A racing heart and sweaty palms are signs of arousal, which can support performance. Studies on reappraisal suggest that interpreting these signals as your body preparing to perform — rather than as a sign of impending failure — can improve outcomes. Try telling yourself, "This is my body getting ready," rather than "I need to calm down."

## During the exam

### A 60-second reset

When you notice panic rising — a run of hard questions, a blank moment, time pressure — use a short reset:

1. Take your eyes off the screen.
2. Breathe in slowly through your nose for about four seconds.
3. Breathe out slowly for about six seconds. Repeat three to five times.
4. Relax your shoulders and jaw.
5. Return to the question and read only the final sentence first.

Longer out-breaths are widely used to slow the body's stress response. The whole reset costs less than a minute and often saves several.

### Expect hard patches

Difficulty is rarely even. Many exams mix question difficulty, and some include unscored items you cannot identify. A run of hard questions does not mean you are failing. Mark them, make your best choice and move on.

### Use a decision rule

Indecision consumes time and confidence. Decide in advance: "If I cannot choose after a set time, I eliminate what I can, pick the best remaining option, flag it and move on." Following a rule is calmer than debating every option.

### Use breaks well

If breaks are allowed, stand, stretch, drink water and do one breathing reset. Avoid replaying questions you found difficult — you cannot change them.

## A pre-exam routine you can rehearse

Routines reduce decisions, and fewer decisions mean less room for worry. Build a short routine and rehearse it before every full mock so it is automatic on the day:

1. **Two hours before:** a familiar meal and a glass of water.
2. **One hour before:** final logistics check — ID, room set-up or travel.
3. **Thirty minutes before:** ten minutes of expressive writing about any worries, then put the paper away.
4. **Ten minutes before:** three slow breathing cycles and one sentence of reappraisal: "This feeling is my body getting ready."
5. **At the start:** read the first question's final sentence first, then the scenario.

By exam day you will have done this several times. Familiarity is one of the most reliable antidotes to anxiety.

## After a difficult exam

Whatever happens, write down what felt hard while it is fresh. If you need to retake, those notes are valuable. And remember that one result does not define your ability: many successful professionals passed on a second attempt after adjusting their preparation.

## When to get extra support

If anxiety is causing you to avoid studying altogether, disrupting sleep for weeks, or producing panic attacks, it is worth talking to a professional. Some exam bodies also offer testing accommodations for documented conditions; check the official requirements and application deadlines of your exam body well in advance.

## Supporting someone else through an exam

If you manage or live with someone preparing for a professional exam, practical help matters more than reassurance. Protect their study time, avoid asking for daily progress reports, help with logistics in the final week and make exam day as ordinary as possible. After the exam, ask how they are rather than how it went.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is some exam anxiety normal?

Yes. Most candidates feel nervous before important exams, and moderate arousal can improve focus. The aim is to keep it at a level where you can think clearly.

### What is the fastest way to calm down during an exam?

A short breathing reset with slow, longer out-breaths, followed by re-reading only the final sentence of the question, works for many people. Practise it during mocks so it is automatic.

### Does more studying reduce anxiety?

More effective studying does — especially retrieval practice and full-length mock exams that make the experience familiar. Long hours of passive re-reading usually do not.

### Can I request accommodations for anxiety?

Some exam bodies offer accommodations for documented conditions. Requirements and deadlines vary, so check your exam body's official policy early.

### Should I avoid caffeine on exam day?

Keep to your normal routine. If you usually drink coffee, a usual amount is fine; a much larger dose than normal can increase jitteriness. Rehearse your exam-day routine, including food and drink, during full mock exams.
