A full-length mock exam is the closest rehearsal you can get to the real thing, which is why candidates often feel a mix of dread and relief when they finish one. Then they check the score, note the weak topics and move on. That is a waste. The score is the least useful output of a mock. The real value lies in the mock exam review: a structured look at why each point was lost.

This guide offers an error taxonomy, a post-mock loop you can repeat, a simple error log and advice on when to retake. It applies to any credential.

> Mock exams are practice tools, and the format, timing, scoring and rules of the real exam are set by the official body for your credential and can change. Check the official body's current requirements so that your mocks match the real conditions as closely as possible.

## Why the score is not the point

A single percentage hides the information you need. Two candidates can both score 62 percent for entirely different reasons: one lacks knowledge of three topics, the other understands the content but misreads questions and runs out of time. They need different fixes. A good review turns the score into a diagnosis.

## An error taxonomy: four causes

Every missed question, and every correct guess, belongs in one of four categories.

### 1. Knowledge errors

You did not know or could not apply the concept, rule or method. The fix is relearning followed by retrieval practice, not rereading.

### 2. Reading errors

You knew the material but misread the stem, missed a qualifier such as not, first or best, or overlooked a detail in an exhibit. The fix is process: underline the task, note qualifiers and check what is being asked before choosing.

### 3. Time errors

You ran short of time, rushed the final questions or spent too long on one item. The fix is pacing: checkpoints, a rule for when to move on and timed practice.

### 4. Nerves and strategy errors

You changed a correct answer without reason, froze, second-guessed or fell for a plausible distractor under stress. The fix is rehearsal under realistic conditions, a calm routine and a rule for when to change an answer.

Sorting errors honestly is uncomfortable, since it is tempting to call everything a silly mistake. A good test: if you could not have answered correctly even with unlimited time and a calm mind, it is a knowledge error.

## The post-mock loop

Follow the same steps after every mock. A good review takes about as long as the exam itself, and it is worth it.

1. **Rest first.** Wait until you can review calmly, but do it within a day or two while the experience is fresh.
2. **Check the score by section and topic,** and by question type.
3. **Review every wrong answer and every guess.** Include questions you got right for the wrong reason.
4. **Classify each by cause** using the four categories.
5. **Write an error-log entry** for each (see below).
6. **Count the causes.** Which category dominates?
7. **Plan the fix.** Choose the two or three actions that will remove the most points.
8. **Retest within a week,** using fresh questions on the weak topics and a short timed set for reading and time errors.

## The error log

An error log is a simple table you maintain throughout your preparation. The entry format matters less than the discipline of using it. An **illustrative** example:

| Date | Topic | What happened | Cause | Fix | Retest date |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 12 May | Revenue allocation | Used stated, not standalone, prices | Knowledge | Rewrite the five steps from memory, do three questions | 16 May |
| 12 May | Control types | Missed the word detective | Reading | Underline qualifiers before choosing | 15 May |
| 12 May | Final testlet | Left four unanswered | Time | Checkpoint at the halfway mark; practise a timed set | 19 May |
| 12 May | Scenario question | Changed a correct answer | Nerves | Rule: change only with a specific reason | 19 May |

### Habits that make the log work

- **Write the reason in your own words,** not just the right answer.
- **Phrase the fix as an action** with a date, not a vague intention.
- **Review the log weekly** and scan it before every mock. Recurring entries are your best predictors of what will go wrong again.
- **Delete or tick off** entries only when you have retested successfully.

## Reviewing the right questions

Do not limit yourself to wrong answers. Include:

- **Lucky guesses,** which look like success but are knowledge gaps.
- **Slow correct answers,** which suggest fragile understanding or a time risk.
- **Questions you flagged,** since flags show where your confidence was low.

This produces a more accurate picture of readiness than the raw score.

## Turning the review into study

Match each error type to a study action.

- **Knowledge:** relearn the concept in a short block, then test yourself the same day and again a few days later. The spacing principles are in [spaced repetition and active recall for professional exams](/blog/spaced-repetition-and-active-recall-for-exams).
- **Reading:** do a set of questions where you only identify the task and the qualifiers, without answering.
- **Time:** run timed mini-sets of ten questions with a target time per question.
- **Nerves:** simulate the exam day: same start time, same breaks, no notes. If you struggle with simulation items, see our [guide to CPA task-based simulations](/blog/cpa-task-based-simulations-guide) for an approach to pacing.

If you are studying around a job, fit these actions into your template using the advice in [studying for a certification while working full time](/blog/study-for-certification-while-working-full-time).

## When to retake a mock

Resist the urge to retake the same mock quickly, because you will remember the questions and the score will mislead you. A better rule:

1. **Fix first,** then practise on new material for at least a week.
2. **Retake a different mock** to measure progress.
3. **Retake the same mock** only after a long gap, and treat it as a review of your errors, not a measure of readiness.
4. **Judge trends, not single scores.** A pattern across two or three mocks is more reliable than one result.
5. **Delay the exam if the trend is flat** and the errors are knowledge errors you have not yet fixed.

## A worked review of a single question

An **illustrative** walk-through shows how the taxonomy works in practice.

*You missed a scenario question asking which action an auditor should take first after discovering unauthorised access. You chose to recommend a company-wide password reset.*

Step one, restate the question in your own words: the task was to take the first action, not the final recommendation. Step two, find the point of failure. Did you know that establishing scope comes before remedies? If you did and still chose the reset, it was a reading error, because you overlooked the word first. If you did not know the sequence, it was a knowledge error. Step three, write the entry with the cause, the fix and the retest date. For a reading error, the fix might be a rule to circle qualifiers; for a knowledge error, it might be to write the sequence of response steps from memory twice in the coming week.

Repeating this for twenty or thirty questions reveals clear patterns. Many candidates discover that one cause accounts for more than half of their lost points, which makes the next two weeks of study far more focused than any generic plan.

### Keep the emotional side in check

A mock exam score can be discouraging, and discouragement leads to skipped reviews. Treat the result as data, not a verdict. A poor mock taken early in preparation is cheap information. A poor mock in the final week is also useful if you have time to act. The only truly bad mock is one that you do not review.

## Reviewing by credential type

The loop is the same everywhere, but what you look for differs.

- **Written-answer exams:** compare your answer with a rubric point by point, as described in our guide to [CMA essay questions](/blog/cma-essay-questions-technique).
- **Long-programme finance exams:** track error causes across several mocks, which is central to a [CFA Level 1 study schedule](/blog/cfa-level-1-study-schedule).
- **Audit and security exams:** note whether misses were about the risk, the control or the evidence, as in [CIA Part 1](/blog/cia-part-1-essentials-of-internal-auditing) and [CISA Domain 5](/blog/cisa-domain-5-protection-of-information-assets).
- **Project management exams:** tag misses by context and principle, as discussed in [PMP agile situational questions](/blog/pmp-agile-situational-questions).
- **Nursing exams:** tag priority and scope errors, using [NCLEX prioritisation frameworks](/blog/nclex-prioritization-frameworks) and [NCLEX delegation questions](/blog/nclex-delegation-questions). These are study techniques only, not clinical guidance.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should a mock exam review take?

Plan for roughly as long as the exam itself, spread over one or two sessions. Rushing the review defeats the purpose of taking the mock.

### Should I review questions I answered correctly?

Yes, at least those you guessed, flagged or answered slowly. Correct answers reached for the wrong reason hide gaps.

### How many mock exams should I take?

Take as many as you can review properly in the time you have. One thoroughly reviewed mock is worth more than several skimmed ones.

### What if my score does not improve?

Look at the error causes. If knowledge errors dominate, relearn and retest. If reading, time or nerves dominate, practise those skills directly. If nothing changes after a few cycles, consider moving the exam date.
