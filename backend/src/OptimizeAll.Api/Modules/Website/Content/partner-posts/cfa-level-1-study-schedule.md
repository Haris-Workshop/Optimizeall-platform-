Most CFA Level 1 study schedules start on day one and march forward through the curriculum, which means the revision, practice and mock exams get squeezed into whatever time is left. A better approach is to plan backwards from exam day. You fix the non-negotiables first: the final review block, the mock exams, the practice questions. Then you see how much time is truly available for first-pass learning.

This guide shows how to build a CFA Level 1 study schedule that way, with an illustrative week-by-week table, practice milestones and a review loop.

> Curriculum content, topic weightings, exam format, registration deadlines, fees and scoring are set by CFA Institute and are revised regularly. Check the official body's current requirements, curriculum and exam dates before you plan. This article stays general on purpose.

## Why plan backwards

Forward planning hides a common trap: the first topics get thorough study, the later ones get rushed, and no time remains to integrate the material. Backward planning avoids that by reserving the end of the calendar for the activities that most improve exam performance.

Work in this order:

1. **Fix the exam date** from the official calendar.
2. **Reserve the final phase** for mock exams and targeted review.
3. **Reserve the middle phase** for question practice on everything you have covered.
4. **Allocate the remainder** to first-pass learning, weighted by topic size and your own weaknesses.
5. **Add buffer weeks** for illness, work and life.

## Step 1: count your real hours

Be honest about the weeks you have and the hours you can sustain. Subtract holidays, work peaks and family commitments. A schedule with a modest, reliable weekly figure beats an ambitious one you abandon in week four. If you work full time, our guide to [studying for a certification while working full time](/blog/study-for-certification-while-working-full-time) shows how to run a time audit and build a weekly template.

## Step 2: weight topics in general terms

CFA Institute publishes topic weightings and they differ across subjects, with some areas carrying markedly more of the exam than others. Use the current published ranges to decide where time goes, but think in broad terms: large topic areas deserve a bigger share of first-pass time, and small areas should not be skipped because every area appears on the exam.

Then adjust for you. Rate each topic as strong, rusty or new, and give more time to new topics, since they need first-time learning, and to rusty ones that carry a high share. Financial reporting concepts, for instance, may be familiar to an accountant but entirely new to an engineer; the reverse may be true for quantitative methods.

## Step 3: an illustrative backwards-planned table

The table assumes a 24-week runway and about 10 to 12 study hours a week. It is **illustrative only**; scale the phases to your own calendar.

| Weeks before exam | Phase | Main activity | Milestone |
|---|---|---|---|
| 24 to 23 | Setup | Download official curriculum map; set targets; first diagnostic set of questions | Baseline score recorded |
| 22 to 14 | First pass | Learn topics in order of size and weakness; questions after each topic within a day or two | Every topic covered once |
| 13 to 12 | Consolidate | Mixed questions across all topics; build error log | Weakest three topics identified |
| 11 to 6 | Second pass | Revisit weak topics; formula and definition retrieval drills; weekly mixed sets | Mixed-set accuracy trending up |
| 5 to 3 | Mock phase | Two to three full-length timed mocks, one per week, each reviewed in depth | Mock review complete |
| 2 | Targeted review | Fix recurring errors; review error log and formula sheet | Error log shrinking |
| 1 | Taper | Light recall, sleep, logistics; no new material | Ready |

Notice that the final six weeks contain no first-pass learning at all. That is the point of working backwards.

## Step 4: structure the week

A repeatable week lowers the effort of deciding what to do. For a candidate with about 10 to 12 hours:

- **Four weekday sessions of 60 to 90 minutes:** one topic block, then questions on that topic.
- **One short session on formulae and definitions:** retrieval only, no rereading.
- **One long weekend block:** a mixed question set under time, then error-log review.
- **One free day.** Rest is part of the plan.

Every session should end with one written line stating what to revisit next.

## Step 5: the review loop

Reading a chapter once produces fast forgetting. A loop that works:

1. **Learn** a topic in a focused block.
2. **Test** yourself the same or next day with questions.
3. **Log** each error with its cause: knowledge, reading, time or calculation.
4. **Revisit** the topic after a few days, then after about a week, then after a few weeks.
5. **Mix** it into cumulative sets so you practise choosing methods, not just applying one.

The principles are explained in [spaced repetition and active recall for professional exams](/blog/spaced-repetition-and-active-recall-for-exams). For more on strategy across the whole programme, see our [CFA exam study strategies](/blog/cfa-exam-study-strategies).

## Practice milestones

Set checkpoints so that you know whether you are on track:

- **End of first pass:** every topic covered at least once, with a question set attempted for each.
- **Mid-point mixed set:** a timed set across all topics, with the results used to rebalance your hours.
- **First full mock:** taken with no notes, in conditions as close to the real exam as possible.
- **Final mock:** about two weeks before the exam, with enough time left to act on what it reveals.

If a milestone slips, do not squeeze everything. Cut lower-weight activities, use buffer weeks or, if the gap is large, consider whether a later sitting is the more sensible choice.

### An illustrative error-log entry

*Topic: financial reporting. Question: revenue recognition on a multi-element contract. Result: wrong. Cause: knowledge, since I applied one allocation step too early. Fix: rewrite the five-step sequence from memory and redo three questions in three days.*

A log of such entries tells you where to spend scarce hours. If revenue questions trip you up, our [ASC 606 and IFRS 15 notes](/blog/asc-606-vs-ifrs-15-study-notes) explain the sequence at a general level.

## Build the formula sheet as you go

Level 1 carries a large number of formulae, and candidates who leave them to the final weeks tend to memorise without understanding. Keep a living formula sheet from week one. For each entry record three things: the formula, the situation that calls for it and one common trap, such as using a nominal rate where a periodic rate is needed. Then drill the sheet in two directions. Given a formula, state when it applies; given a short scenario, name the formula. The second direction is what the exam tests, and it is the one most candidates skip. Keep the sheet to a few pages, prune anything you can now derive from memory and date each revision so that your final-week version is short.

## What to do in the final week

Lighten the load. Review your error log, skim your formula sheet and test recall of key definitions. Check logistics for exam day: identification, route, timing and allowed materials, all confirmed with the official instructions. Sleep matters more than another late-night question set.

## Adjusting the schedule when life intervenes

No plan survives contact with real life unchanged, so decide your adjustment rules in advance.

- **If you fall one week behind,** use a buffer week rather than compressing later phases.
- **If you fall two or more weeks behind,** cut the lowest-value activity first, such as rereading or re-formatting notes, and protect practice questions and mocks.
- **If a mock reveals a large gap in one area,** shift hours from areas where your mixed-set results are consistently strong.
- **If motivation dips,** shorten sessions but keep the streak. Twenty minutes of retrieval is better than a skipped day.

### A simple weekly check-in

Every Sunday, spend ten minutes answering four questions. What did I plan and what did I complete? Which three errors recurred? What will I change next week? Which topic needs an extra retrieval session? Writing the answers in a few lines keeps the plan alive and gives you data for the later phases. It also makes it much easier to decide calmly, rather than anxiously, whether the exam date is still realistic.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many weeks should a CFA Level 1 study schedule run?

It depends on your background and weekly hours. Estimate the total hours you need by topic, divide by the hours you can reliably study and add buffer. Plan the last six weeks or so for practice and mocks.

### Should I study topics in curriculum order?

Not necessarily. Many candidates start with the largest or most unfamiliar topics so they get the most time, but you can follow the curriculum order if that suits you better. What matters is that every topic is covered and reviewed.

### How many mock exams should I take?

Enough to learn from, with time to review each one properly. Two or three full-length mocks in the closing weeks is a common pattern, but a reviewed mock is worth more than several unreviewed ones.

### Where do I find official topic weightings and dates?

On CFA Institute's website. Always use the current published information.
