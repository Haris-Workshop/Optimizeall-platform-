Multiple-choice questions test whether you recognise a correct answer. CPA task-based simulations test whether you can do the work. You are handed a scenario, a set of exhibits and a task, and you must produce an answer: complete a schedule, classify items, locate the relevant guidance, or evaluate whether a document supports a conclusion. Candidates who prepare only with multiple-choice questions often find simulations slower and more tiring than they expected.

This guide explains what simulations are testing, offers a repeatable approach for the common types, shows how to manage the clock, and works through an illustrative example.

> Exam format, item types, scoring and the weight given to simulations are set by the AICPA and may change. Check the official bodies' current requirements and the sample tests they publish before you rely on any description here.

## What simulations are really testing

Simulations assess application. They sit at the upper end of the skills the exam looks for: not just remembering a rule but selecting the right one, applying it to facts and presenting a result. In practice that means three abilities:

- **Reading precisely.** Exhibits contain the facts, and small details change the answer.
- **Selecting the right framework.** You need to know which standard or procedure governs the situation.
- **Executing cleanly.** Calculations and entries must be accurate and consistent across the parts of the task.

Because partial work can earn credit in some formats while errors can carry through others, a methodical approach protects you from cascading mistakes.

## A universal approach in five steps

Whatever the topic, the same routine works.

1. **Read the requirement first.** Know what you must produce before you look at the exhibits. This stops you drowning in detail you do not need.
2. **Skim all exhibits once.** Note what each contains and where it sits. Do not start calculating yet.
3. **Map exhibits to requirements.** For each part of the task, write a quick note on which exhibit supplies the facts.
4. **Work in the order that earns marks fastest.** Do independent, confident parts first. Return to the hard parts once the rest is secure.
5. **Check before you submit.** Confirm totals foot, signs are right and your answers agree with each other.

## Approaches by simulation type

Simulations vary, but most fall into a few broad families. The detail below is general; confirm current formats with the AICPA.

### Calculation and schedule completion

You fill in amounts in a table or worksheet, often building from supplied data. Set out your working in a clear layout, take every figure from the exhibit rather than memory, and keep a running check such as a total that must equal a balance.

### Classification and matching

You decide where items belong: which category, which financial statement line, or which assertion is addressed. Work from definitions, not instinct, and go through each item against a short checklist.

### Document review

You read a memo, a draft report or a set of notes and decide whether statements are supported or what is missing. Treat each sentence as a claim to test against the facts in the exhibits.

### Research

You locate guidance in a supplied database to support a conclusion. Practise the habit of searching with the right terms, reading the exact paragraph and quoting the reference format precisely.

### Journal entries and reconciliations

You prepare entries or reconcile balances. Use a consistent order, such as debits first, and check that the entry balances before moving on.

## An illustrative worked example

The scenario below is **fictional and for illustration only**.

*Northwind Ltd signs a one-year contract with a customer for equipment and 12 months of support. The exhibits show the contract price, the standalone selling prices of the equipment and the support, the delivery date, and the start date of the support. Task: determine the revenue to recognise for the equipment at delivery.*

A strong approach:

1. **Requirement:** revenue for the equipment at delivery. That is a narrow target.
2. **Framework:** this is a multiple-element arrangement, so apply the five-step revenue model: identify the contract, identify the performance obligations, determine the transaction price, allocate it on relative standalone selling prices, and recognise revenue as each obligation is satisfied.
3. **Map exhibits:** contract price from the contract; standalone prices from the pricing schedule; delivery date from the shipping record.
4. **Work:** suppose, purely for illustration, the contract price is 11,000, the standalone price of the equipment is 10,000 and of the support is 2,000. The total of standalone prices is 12,000, so the equipment receives 10,000 divided by 12,000 of 11,000, which is about 9,167. The support receives about 1,833, recognised over the support period.
5. **Check:** 9,167 plus 1,833 equals 11,000. The equipment portion is recognised at the point control transfers, shown by the delivery record.

Notice that the structure did most of the work. If a part of the task asked about the support revenue after three months, you would reuse the same allocation rather than start again. ## Managing time

Simulations are expensive in minutes, so decide your pacing in advance.

- **Know the testlet structure.** Check the current format so you understand how time is shared between question types.
- **Set a checkpoint for each simulation.** If you have spent your allowance and are stuck, record your best answer, flag it and move on.
- **Resist perfectionism on the first pass.** A complete, mostly correct simulation is worth more than one perfect part and one blank.
- **Reserve a few minutes at the end** for flagged items and for checks such as totals and signs.

Practise with a timer from the start. A candidate who has only ever solved simulations without a clock is meeting two difficulties at once on exam day.

## How to practise them

- **Do at least one simulation in every study week**, starting early in your preparation.
- **Redo, do not reread.** After a miss, close the solution and rebuild the answer from the exhibits a few days later.
- **Classify every error.** Was it knowledge, reading, execution or time? Our [mock exam review method](/blog/how-to-review-a-mock-exam) provides an error taxonomy.
- **Write a one-line method note** for each type, such as how you structure a reconciliation, and reread those notes before practice sessions.

## Build exhibit-reading speed

Much of the time in a simulation goes on reading, so train that skill separately. Take a practice simulation and, before answering, give yourself three minutes to produce a one-line summary of each exhibit: what it is, which figures it contains and which part of the requirement it serves. Then answer the task. Over a few weeks this exhibit map becomes a reflex and the answering phase gets faster, because you no longer re-read documents hunting for a number. Also practise with the on-screen tools you will have in the real exam, such as the spreadsheet and the authoritative literature search, as described in the AICPA's sample tests, so that the interface does not cost you time on the day.

## Mistakes that cost marks

- **Starting to calculate before reading the requirement.** You may solve a problem the task never asked.
- **Copying numbers from memory of the exhibit.** Always return to the source line; transposition slips are common under time pressure.
- **Letting an early error cascade.** If a later part depends on an earlier figure, double-check the earlier figure before building on it.
- **Over-researching.** In a research task, once you find the paragraph that supports the answer, record it and move on.
- **Skipping the final check.** Thirty seconds spent confirming that totals agree and signs make sense catches many avoidable errors.

A useful habit is to keep a short personal checklist of your own recurring slips, such as forgetting to convert units or applying the wrong period. Read it before each practice simulation and each real testlet.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are task-based simulations harder than multiple-choice questions?

They are different rather than simply harder. They demand application and take longer, so they feel harder to candidates who have practised mostly with multiple-choice items.

### Should I do simulations first or last in a testlet?

That depends on the current format and your strengths. Practise both orders in mock conditions and use whichever lets you bank confident marks early without starving the harder items of time.

### What if I do not know the answer to part of a simulation?

Complete everything you can, make a reasoned attempt at the rest and move on. Partial progress usually beats leaving a section empty, but check the current instructions for how the exam handles it.

### How many simulations should I practise?

Aim for steady weekly practice across all types rather than a fixed number. Quality of review matters more than the count.
